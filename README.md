# Social-Media-and-Billboard-Chart-Performance
Introduction
The modern music industry has been strongly influenced by the rise of social media and streaming platforms. These applications often play a significant role in determining which songs are discovered and become popular. The article “Music is Just Right There on Social Media!: Discovering, Exploring,and Incorporating Songs Across Platforms” discusses how algorithms on platforms such as TikTok, YouTube, and Spotify are pivotal in the process of music discovery and consumption. This constitutes the basis of this study which will focus on streaming and engagement metrics as predictors for hit songs. The study “Dance Hit Song Prediction" is also within the field of music analysis and aims to classify songs into categories from music features by applying machine learning models. Similarly, this study will incorporate classification techniques, with the most significant difference being that streams and virality on platforms will be the predictors for a song’s success rather than audio features such as the earlier study uses. This analysis will be a relevant contribution to developing the field of “Hit Song Science (HSS)” exploring different predictors that are more in line with an ever evolving music industry. 

With the help of social media, users can discover new music and content more easily than ever. In the article "The Use of Social Media for Artist Marketing: Music Industry Perspectives and Consumer Motivations", researchers investigated the question of why consumers use social media to engage with music, and how companies can improve their marketing by adjusting to consumer needs. The study revealed five main motives for social media users in the context of music: access to content, sense of affinity, participation, interaction, and social identity (in descending order). According to their findings, social media users prioritize discovering new content on social media, as opposed to interacting with others or building communities. This paper’s analysis builds upon this conclusion, as it explores the question of whether a song’s performance on social media can predict its all-time rank. 

An article which focuses on the effects of social media’s expansion on the music industry as a whole is "The online place of popular music: Exploring the impact of geography and social media on pop artists’ mainstream media attention" from 2016. At this time, popular artists such as Radiohead, Arctic Monkeys, and Lady Gaga were cited as pivoting to having a higher social media presence to counteract the blurring of lines between professional and user-made music. This observed effect, where artists, both professional and casual, started appearing more on social media, can be seen as the main driver for intrigue in this paper; if artists have already started to focus on social media presence, are we able to see whether that social media influence has an impact on the success of their music?

In this research, there will be an investigation into the effect of streaming and engagement metrics on different platforms on a song’s popularity. This report will investigate two research questions: (1) Can we classify a song as a long-running hit (more than 20 weeks on the chart) or a short-lived hit (less than 20 weeks) using its Spotify Streams, YouTube Playlist reach, TikTok posts, single status, all time rank, the month of release, and the peak position on the Billboard Hot 100? and (2) Can we predict a song’s All-Time Rank using YouTube Likes, TikTok Likes, Spotify Popularity Score, Weeks on Chart, Single Status, Billboard Rank, and Month of Release? Through our analysis, we hope to gain a deeper understanding of what causes a song to perform better on the chart. This knowledge will allow those within the music industry the ability to discern what contributes most to a song’s success which will assist with music marketing. Through these questions, we will determine how previous trends we identified can allow us to make predictions that are relevant in an analysis of how the modern music industry functions.

Data

The first dataset we utilized within this study is collected by researcher Maureen Ekwebelem from Fordham University who compiled engagement metrics from multiple platforms such as Spotify, YouTube, TikTok, SoundCloud, and Amazon. The second dataset we used was data scraped from the Billboard Hot 100 Chart. These two datasets were merged by Track name. The final data had about 4830 observations and 24 variables. The data spans the years 2022 to 2025.

Within this dataset, our research focused on a select number of variables that had the potential to be strong predictors of a song’s chart performance. The engagement metrics collected from different platforms most popular with the general public comprise the majority of the relevant variables. One such platform is Spotify with the corresponding variables of Spotify Streams, number of streams a song has, and Spotify Playlist Count, which refers to the number of Spotify playlists the song is in. In addition, the data contains metrics for YouTube and TikTok. YouTube Views and YouTube Likes indicate the number of likes and views the song has garnered on YouTube while YouTube Playlist Reach signifies the number of followers of YouTube playlists that include the track. TikTok Posts, TikTok Likes, and TikTok Views represent comparable metrics but for TikTok. All Time Rank is the overall rank of songs by popularity within the dataset. Single Status is a derived label not within the original data, created by checking whether the Track name appears within the Album Name, as singles are released with the same name as the song. We also
engineered the feature Month of Release by extracting the month from the Release Date. Regarding the data scraped from the Billboard Hot 100, the most significant variables are the Weeks on Chart referring to the number of weeks that a song spends on the chart, and Peak Position.

Due to the merging of two different datasets, there were numerous songs in the dataset that did not have a corresponding observation in the Billboard Hot 100 dataset. This led to quite a few missing values for variables scraped from Billboard. Since the research is focused on Billboard performance, songs that did not appear on a Billboard Hot 100 chart that we scraped were dropped. Therefore, the updated dataset used for our analysis has about 1321 observations. Additionally, certain songs had multiple rows within the dataset because they appeared on the Billboard Hot 100 multiple times during the time period we scraped. This poses a problem when building our machine learning models because of the potential of data leakage. Songs that appear multiple times in the data may appear in the training set and testing set when performing a data split which would affect our model performance metrics. In order to prevent this, we combined the duplicate appearances and split at the song level, guaranteeing that the same song would not be in both the training and testing set. The dataset was also cleaned prior to analysis because of a few observations that did not render properly in text and presented as symbols that were not interpretable. These were dropped for the
sake of readableness.
